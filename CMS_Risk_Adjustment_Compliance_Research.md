# CMS Regulatory Requirements and Compliance for Risk Adjustment
## Comprehensive Research Report

**Report Date:** April 1, 2026  
**Subject:** Risk Adjustment Factor (RAF) Compliance, RADV Audits, and CMS Enforcement

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Medicare Advantage (MA) risk adjustment is undergoing intense regulatory scrutiny, with CMS expanding audits from 60 to 550+ plans while growing their coding teams from 40 to 2,000 reviewers. The DOJ has recovered over $6.8 billion in healthcare fraud settlements (FY 2025) with significant focus on MA risk adjustment. A federal court vacated CMS's 2023 RADV extrapolation rule in September 2025, creating procedural uncertainty even as enforcement pressure intensifies. Organizations must implement comprehensive compliance frameworks addressing data accuracy, documentation standards, coder credentials, and encounter data completeness.

## 1. RADV AUDITS: METHODOLOGY, SCOPE, AND PENALTIES

### 1.1 Overview and Legal Framework

**Regulatory Citation:** 42 CFR § 422.310 (Risk adjustment data)

**Key Authority:** CMS-1733-F (February 1, 2023 Final Rule - currently vacated as of September 25, 2025)

CMS conducts Risk Adjustment Data Validation (RADV) audits to ensure MA organizations submit accurate diagnosis codes supporting higher capitated payments. RADV audits examine whether the diagnosis codes submitted by MA organizations are:
- Supported by documentation in medical records
- Accurately coded according to ICD-10-CM guidelines
- Eligible for the claimed Hierarchical Condition Category (HCC)

### 1.2 Audit Sample Size and Coverage

**Current Expansion (May 2025):**
- CMS expanded from auditing 60 Medicare Advantage plans to targeting 550+ eligible MA contracts
- Coding review teams expanded from 40 to 2,000+ reviewers
- Timeline: Complete backlog of PY 2018-2024 audits by early 2026

**Payment Years Under Audit:**
- PY 2018-2024 initially targeted (as of November 2024)
- Plans initiated without FFS Adjuster and subject to extrapolation methodology

### 1.3 Extrapolation Methodology and Financial Impact

**Current Status:** The 2023 Final Rule authorizing extrapolation was vacated on September 25, 2025 (Humana Inc. v. Becerra), but CMS appealed November 21, 2025. The methodology remains in flux.

**How Extrapolation Works:**
Under the vacated 2023 Final Rule, CMS calculates an error rate from an audit sample, then applies that percentage to all similar payments across the entire contract. Using advanced statistical models, a modest percentage error in sampled charts could extrapolate to millions in recoupment.

**Financial Impact Examples:**
- A 5% error rate on a sample could generate millions in recoupment demands
- CMS estimated recovering $4.7 billion through extrapolation between 2023-2032
- Small documentation errors now risk extrapolating into massive penalties affecting thousands of members

**Industry Estimates:**
- CMS estimates MA plans overbill by $17 billion annually
- Medicare Payment Advisory Commission (MedPAC) estimates overpayments as high as $43 billion

### 1.4 FFS Adjuster Controversy

**FFS Normalization Factor (2025):**
CMS finalized a multiple linear regression methodology for calculating normalization factors for CMS-HCC models, incorporating the most recent average FFS risk scores without excluding years while making reasonable projections of actual average FFS risk scores in the payment year.

**Elimination of FFS Adjuster (2023 Rule):**
- CMS rejected proposals to incorporate an FFS Adjuster into RADV methodology
- Without FFS Adjuster, every unsupported HCC is treated as 100% overpayment
- No built-in tolerance for normal documentation variance
- Creates asymmetry: FFS Medicare claims not subjected to same audit level as MA data

**Humana v. Becerra Court Challenge:**
Judge Reed O'Connor vacated the 2023 Final Rule on Administrative Procedure Act (APA) grounds, finding that CMS's reasoning for eliminating the FFS adjuster was not a "logical outgrowth" of the 2018 Proposed Rule. The court did not address the substantive legality of extrapolation or elimination of the FFS adjuster, leaving uncertainty pending CMS's appeal.

### 1.5 Audit Selection Criteria

CMS initially began auditing contracts covering Payment Year 2018 and subsequent years. Plans are selected based on:
- Contract size and payment amounts
- Historical coding patterns and risk scores
- Prior audit results showing high error rates
- Specific high-risk diagnosis code patterns identified by OIG

## 2. OIG ENFORCEMENT ACTIONS ON RAF FRAUD

### 2.1 OIG Authority and Enforcement Tools

**Statutory Authority:**
- Social Security Act § 1128A (Civil Monetary Penalties)
- Social Security Act § 1128(b) (Exclusion Authority)
- 42 CFR Part 402 (Civil Money Penalties, Assessments, and Exclusions)

**OIG Enforcement Mechanisms:**
1. Civil Monetary Penalties (CMP) - up to $25,000 per affected beneficiary
2. Assessments - additional civil penalties
3. Exclusion from Federal healthcare programs (LEIE - List of Excluded Individuals/Entities)
4. Exclusion screening requirements: Monthly for MA Plans per 42 CFR Part 422

### 2.2 OIG's Risk Adjustment Focus

**December 2023 OIG Toolkit:**
OIG published a toolkit with SQL scripts for identifying suspicious HCC coding practices. The toolkit identifies high-risk diagnosis code groups that were consistently submitted to CMS with insufficient documentation.

**Current OIG Work Plan Items:**
- "Medicare Advantage Risk-Adjustment Data - Targeted Review of Documentation Supporting Specific Diagnosis Codes" (OIG WP-00-24-35079)
- Audit of diagnosis codes submitted by MA organizations for risk adjustment program use
- Trends, patterns, and key comparisons between CMS-HCC models

### 2.3 OIG Compliance Program Guidance (February 2026)

**First Major Update Since 1999:**
OIG issued new Industry Compliance Program Guidance (ICPG) on February 3, 2026, representing the first comprehensive update to MA compliance expectations in 25+ years.

**Key Risk Areas Identified:**
1. Risk Adjustment (primary focus)
2. Access to Care
3. Marketing and Enrollment
4. Third-party Oversight
5. Data Integrity

**Recommended Compliance Elements for Risk Adjustment:**
- Pre- and post-submission data audits
- Special scrutiny for high-risk HCC codes
- Paired diagnosis capture programs with clinical quality controls
- Provider and coder education programs
- Filtering logic and analytics to detect coding anomalies
- Benchmarking HCC prevalence and risk scores against national standards
- Auditing vendors and providers receiving risk-based incentives
- Prompt corrective action and overpayment reporting to CMS

### 2.4 Exclusion List (LEIE) Requirements

**Regulatory Requirement:**
MA plans must conduct monthly screening of providers, suppliers, and practitioners against the OIG's List of Excluded Individuals/Entities (LEIE).

**Consequences of Hiring Excluded Individuals/Entities:**
- Organizations may be subject to civil monetary penalties
- Automatic overpayment liability for services furnished by excluded parties
- Potential exclusion of the hiring organization itself for failure to screen

**Bases for Exclusion Related to Risk Adjustment Fraud:**
- Conviction for Medicare/Medicaid fraud
- Suspension or revocation of professional license
- Provision of unnecessary or substandard services
- Submission of false or fraudulent claims to Federal healthcare programs
- Pattern of billing inconsistent with patient needs

## 3. FALSE CLAIMS ACT (FCA) IMPLICATIONS

### 3.1 Legal Framework

**Governing Statute:** 31 U.S.C. § 3729 (False Claims Act)

**Key Provisions:**
- Prohibition on knowingly presenting false or fraudulent claims to the government for payment
- Liability extends to persons who "cause to submit" false claims
- Applies to diagnostic coding in risk adjustment submissions

**Qui Tam (Whistleblower) Provisions:**
- Private parties can bring actions on behalf of the government
- Whistleblower compensation: percentage of recovery (typically 15-30%)
- Example: Kaiser case awarded $95 million to whistleblowers from $556 million settlement

### 3.2 FCA Liability Standards for RAF

**Government Position on Risk Adjustment Fraud:**
Submitting unsupported diagnostic codes in an attempt to reap higher capitated rates constitutes false claims under the FCA. This includes:

1. **Upcoding:** Assigning high-dollar HCCs for diagnoses where encounter notes do not indicate severe diagnosis
2. **False Condition Reporting:** Reporting conditions with high HCCs where the conditions have actually resolved
3. **Post-Encounter Addenda:** Making addenda to charts months or years after the encounter to report unsupported HCC codes
4. **Failure to Remove Codes:** Not deleting inappropriate codes that remain on problem lists
5. **One-Way Audits:** Only adding diagnosis codes and never removing them (the "Kaiser Practice")

### 3.3 Penalties and Damages

**FCA Damages Framework:**
- Treble damages: Three times the government's actual damages
- Civil penalties per violation: $11,000-$15,000 per claim (adjusted annually under 45 CFR Part 102)
- Interest on overpayments

**Example Penalty Calculation:**
- If 500,000 unsupported diagnoses = 500,000 false claims
- At $11,000-$15,000 per claim = $5.5-$7.5 billion in penalties alone
- Plus treble damages on actual overpayments

### 3.4 One-Way Audit Prohibition

**DOJ Position:**
The Department of Justice explicitly prohibits "one-way audits" where MA organizations aggressively add diagnosis codes (sometimes months or years post-encounter) but never remove them. This practice was central to Kaiser's settlement.

**Kaiser Settlement Example:**
Kaiser allegedly added approximately 500,000 diagnoses through post-encounter addenda, many months or a year after the visits, generating approximately $1 billion in unsupported payments. The DOJ characterized this as deliberate manipulation of medical records.

### 3.5 Liability Extension to Providers and Coders

**Joint Liability:**
- MA organizations are liable for all risk adjustment data they submit, whether codes originated from providers or from the MA organization's own chart review
- Providers may also face FCA liability if they knowingly submit false diagnoses
- Coders and consultants can face personal liability for deliberate misrepresentation

**Qui Tam Exposure:**
- Any healthcare professional (physician, nurse, coder) can bring a qui tam action
- Recent cases show whistleblower cooperation with DOJ leads to largest settlements

## 4. DOCUMENTATION REQUIREMENTS FOR HCC CODES

### 4.1 Regulatory Documentation Standards

**MEAT Documentation Framework:**
Medicare requires that documentation supporting HCC codes include evidence of:
- **M**onitoring: Active ongoing monitoring of the condition
- **E**valuation: Clinical evaluation and assessment of the condition
- **A**ssessment: Documented clinical assessment showing the condition is present
- **T**reatment: Treatment plan linked to the disease process

**CMS Requirement:**
If conditions are not actively requiring monitoring, ongoing evaluation and assessment with treatment, they are not codable per CMS and the OIG.

### 4.2 Chronic Condition Documentation

**Annual Recertification Requirement:**
- Chronic conditions must be evaluated annually
- Every chronic condition must be documented fresh each calendar year to maintain HCC eligibility
- Treatment needs must be directly linked to the disease process
- Documentation must reflect current clinical status, not historical conditions

### 4.3 Encounter Data Documentation

**42 CFR § 422.310 Requirements:**
MA organizations must submit data that:
1. Conform to CMS' requirements for data equivalent to Medicare fee-for-service data
2. Characterize the context and purposes of each item and service provided
3. Are submitted electronically to the appropriate CMS contractor
4. Meet all relevant national standards

**Submission Deadlines:**
- First Friday in September: Data for services from prior 12 months ending June 30
- First Friday in March: Data for services from prior 12 months ending December 31
- Data submitted after final deadline not used in payment calculations

### 4.4 RADV Chart Review Standards

**Historical Audit Results:**
- Insufficient MEAT documentation invalidated 20-40% of HCCs in sampled charts during prior RADV audits
- Common deficiencies: Missing treatment plans, no evidence of ongoing monitoring, resolution of condition not documented

**Supporting Documentation Expectations:**
- Clinical notes documenting the specific diagnosis
- Relevant test results, imaging, or diagnostic studies
- Treatment records demonstrating active management
- For chronic conditions: Documentation from multiple encounter dates in the year
- Problem list with active status indicators

### 4.5 High-Risk HCC Categories (OIG Identified)

**OIG's December 2023 Toolkit Identified High-Risk Areas:**
1. Diabetes complications (particularly with other HCCs)
2. Heart failure and cardiovascular conditions
3. Chronic kidney disease stages
4. Respiratory/COPD conditions
5. Neurological conditions
6. Morbid obesity (requires BMI documentation)
7. Mental health/behavioral diagnoses

**Scrutiny Level:** These codes receive heightened audit scrutiny and require more robust documentation

## 5. CMS FINAL RULES AFFECTING RISK ADJUSTMENT (2024-2026)

### 5.1 CMS-HCC Risk Adjustment Model Phase-In

**2024 CMS-HCC Model (V28) Implementation:**

**Model Changes:**
- Number of HCCs increased from 86 (V24) to 115 (V28)
- Removed 2,294 ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes that previously mapped to payment HCCs
- Classifies ~74,000 ICD-10 diagnoses into 266 CMS-HCCs
- Disease-specific expansions in heart disease, neurological, blood disorders, kidney disease, vascular disease

**Phase-In Timeline (Finalized):**
- **CY 2024:** 67% current model + 33% new V28 model
- **CY 2025:** 33% current model + 67% new V28 model  
- **CY 2026 (Current):** 100% V28 CMS-HCC model

### 5.2 FFS Normalization Factor (CY 2025-2026)

**Regulatory Citation:** CMS Final Rule on payment methodologies

**Methodology Change:**
CMS finalized a multiple linear regression methodology (implemented CY 2025) for calculating normalization factors that:
- Incorporates most recent average FFS risk scores
- Excludes no years from the calculation
- Makes reasonable projections of actual average FFS risk scores for the payment year
- Reduces reliance on historical averages

**Purpose:** Ensures V28 model adequately normalizes for differences between MA and FFS populations

### 5.3 PACE Organization Transition (CY 2026)

**Regulatory Change:**
For CY 2026, PACE organizations transition to V28 model using blended approach:
- 10% of risk score calculated using 2024 CMS-HCC model
- 90% of risk score calculated using 2017 CMS-HCC model
- Longer phase-in period for PACE due to smaller population and different service model

### 5.4 Coding Intensity Reduction in V28

**Key V28 Design Feature:**
V28 excludes or constrains coefficients of HCCs identified as subject to:
- Intentional discretionary coding variation
- Unintentional discretionary coding by health plans/providers
- Inappropriate coding

**Codes Removed/Constrained:**
- Risk adjustment codes with excessive coding variation eliminated
- Coefficients of remaining high-variance codes reduced
- Strategy aims to improve payment accuracy and reduce MA coding intensity relative to FFS

### 5.5 ICD-10 Terminology Updates (CY 2026)

**Technical Changes:**
- Definition of HCCs updated to remove reference to specific ICD version
- Terminology changed from "disease codes" to "diagnosis codes"
- Changed from "disease groupings" to "diagnosis groupings"
- Ensures consistency with current ICD-10 terminology standards

### 5.6 Risk Adjustment Data Updates (2026)

**Regulatory Amendments:**
- Finalized provisions relating to risk adjustment data updates
- Updated HCC definitions
- Clarified submission requirements under 42 CFR § 422.310(b)

## 6. RADV EXTRAPOLATION METHODOLOGY AND FINANCIAL IMPACT

### 6.1 How Extrapolation Works

**Statistical Methodology:**
Under the vacated 2023 CMS-1733-F Final Rule, CMS employs statistically valid methodologies to:
1. Sample medical records from MA contract enrollees
2. Calculate error rate in sampled charts (% of unsupported diagnoses)
3. Apply error rate to entire contract population
4. Calculate alleged overpayment across all similar payments

**Example Calculation:**
- Contract has 100,000 members
- CMS audits 100 members' records (0.1% sample)
- Finds 5% of HCCs unsupported = $500,000 in sample overpayment
- Extrapolates: 5% × all members' payments = $50 million recoupment demand

### 6.2 Financial Impact on Plans

**Potential Recoupments:**
- A 5% error rate on a small sample could generate millions in recoupment demands
- Small documentation errors now risk creating multimillion-dollar penalties
- Plans reported uncertainty regarding potential exposure across 2018-2024 payment years

**Revised CMS Estimates (as of May 2025):**
- Estimated annual MA overbilling: $17 billion
- Total RADV recovery projection (2023-2032): $4.7 billion

**MedPAC Analysis:**
- Alternative estimate of MA overpayments: up to $43 billion annually
- Suggests CMS estimates may be conservative

### 6.3 Contract-Level Impact

**Contracts Most at Risk:**
- Contracts with rapid HCC growth exceeding FFS benchmarks
- Contracts with high prevalence of discretionary-coding HCCs
- Contracts with prior RADV audit findings
- Contracts with aggressive post-encounter diagnosis capture

**Payment Year Lookback:**
- CMS pursuing audits back to PY 2018
- Plans potentially liable for 6+ years of alleged overpayments
- Retroactive payments affecting current year budgets

### 6.4 September 2025 Court Ruling Impact

**Humana Inc. v. Becerra Decision:**
- Federal court vacated extrapolation rule on Administrative Procedure Act grounds
- Ruling does not eliminate extrapolation authority, only this rule's procedural validity
- Procedurally invalid because CMS failed to adequately explain departure from FFS adjuster reasoning in 2018 Proposed Rule

**Ongoing Uncertainty:**
- CMS appealed November 21, 2025
- Extrapolation methodology remains in limbo pending appeal outcome
- Plans must prepare for both scenarios: extrapolation allowed or extrapolation prohibited

## 7. COMPLIANCE PROGRAM REQUIREMENTS FOR MA ORGANIZATIONS

### 7.1 Regulatory Requirements

**Statutory Basis:** 42 CFR § 422.501 requires MA organizations to maintain a compliance program

**Seven Core Elements (OIG Framework):**
1. Written policies and procedures
2. Designated compliance officer/team
3. Training and education programs
4. Internal monitoring and auditing
5. Reporting mechanisms and investigation procedures
6. Corrective action and remediation processes
7. Enforcement through disciplinary guidelines

### 7.2 Risk Adjustment-Specific Compliance Elements (OIG 2026 Guidance)

**OIG's New MA Compliance Guidance (February 3, 2026):**
MA organizations should implement risk adjustment-specific controls including:

**Data Quality Controls:**
- Pre-submission data audits (before RAPS/EDS submission)
- Post-submission data audits (after CMS processing)
- Analytics-based filtering logic to detect coding anomalies
- Validation of encounter data completeness and accuracy

**Clinical and Coding Controls:**
- Special scrutiny for high-risk HCC codes identified by OIG
- Paired diagnosis capture programs with clinical quality controls
- Separation of duties: clinical staff identify diagnoses, independent coders assign codes
- Code validation against medical record documentation

**Education and Oversight:**
- Provider and coder education on accurate coding and documentation
- Regular training on compliance expectations
- Training on distinction between legitimate capture and aggressive coding
- Clear communication of organizational compliance policies

**Benchmarking and Monitoring:**
- Benchmarking HCC prevalence against national standards and peers
- Monitoring for unusual coding patterns or spikes
- Trend analysis year-over-year
- Member-level analysis for coding consistency

**Third-Party Oversight:**
- Auditing vendors and providers receiving risk-based incentives
- Oversight of vendors providing diagnosis capture services
- Validation that vendors' methods comply with regulatory standards
- Monitoring of payment incentives that could drive inappropriate coding

**Corrective Action:**
- Prompt investigation of identified issues
- Implementation of corrective action plans
- Timely overpayment reporting to CMS
- Documentation of all compliance activities

### 7.3 Written Policies Requirements

**42 CFR § 422.503(b)(4):**
MA organizations must have written policies that:
1. Are regularly reviewed and updated
2. Are distributed to all workforce members
3. Are understood and implemented by all personnel
4. Address the specific compliance risks of risk adjustment

**Policy Content for Risk Adjustment:**
- Prohibition of one-way audits
- Requirements for MEAT documentation support
- Standards for encounter data accuracy and completeness
- Procedures for handling identified compliance issues
- Procedures for timely correction and overpayment reporting
- Anti-retaliation provisions for compliance concerns

### 7.4 Compliance Officer Requirements

**Responsibilities:**
- Report directly to senior management and governing body
- Recommend compliance program improvements
- Oversee compliance training
- Coordinate internal monitoring and auditing
- Manage investigation of compliance concerns
- Ensure prompt corrective action and overpayment reporting

**Risk Adjustment Specific Duties:**
- Monitor risk adjustment coding trends
- Identify high-risk diagnosis codes and practices
- Coordinate with clinical and coding departments
- Manage relationship with OIG regarding compliance questions

### 7.5 Disciplinary Standards and Enforcement

**Guidelines for Enforcement:**
- Clear escalation procedures for violations
- Proportionate sanctions based on severity
- Consistent application across the organization
- Documentation of all disciplinary actions

**Risk Adjustment Violations:**
- Submitting unsupported diagnoses: Potential termination
- One-way audit practices: Potential termination, exclusion referral
- Intentional miscoding: Termination and potential criminal referral
- Negligent coding practices: Retraining, suspension, or termination depending on severity

## 8. CHART REVIEW ATTESTATION AND CODER CREDENTIAL REQUIREMENTS

### 8.1 Coder Credential Standards

**Industry Standard Credentials:**

**CPC (Certified Professional Coder)**
- Issuing Organization: AAPC (American Association of Professional Coders)
- Focus: Outpatient coding including CPT, HCPCS, ICD-10-CM
- Relevance: Primary credential for outpatient and physician office settings

**CCS (Certified Coding Specialist)**
- Issuing Organization: AHIMA (American Health Information Management Association)
- Focus: Hospital-based coding and medical data classification
- Relevance: Inpatient and complex hospital scenarios

**CRC (Certified Risk Adjustment Coder)**
- Issuing Organization: AAPC
- Focus: Risk adjustment coding specific to HCC, CDPS, HHS Risk Adjustment models
- Relevance: Highest relevance for Medicare Advantage chart review and diagnosis code assignment
- Knowledge Areas: ICD-10-CM specific to risk adjustment models, HCC hierarchies, member context

**Minimum Credentials:**
- OIG guidance recommends coders working in risk adjustment hold CRC or equivalent risk adjustment-specific certification
- CPC or CCS acceptable if supplemented with risk adjustment-specific training and continuing education

### 8.2 Chart Review Attestation Requirements

**Regulatory Basis:**
- 42 CFR § 422.310 requires verification of data accuracy through sample medical record submission
- CMS audit protocols require chart review validation

**Attestation Standards:**
Coders must attest that:
1. Medical record documentation supports the diagnosis codes assigned
2. Diagnosis meets the active, currently treated condition standard
3. MEAT documentation is present (Monitoring, Evaluation, Assessment, Treatment)
4. Code assignment follows ICD-10-CM guidelines and CMS conventions
5. HCC hierarchy rules were properly applied
6. No unsupported or post-encounter codes were added

**Attestation Documentation:**
- Signed chart review forms with coder identification
- Date of review and supporting documentation reviewed
- Specific documentation citations supporting each code
- Reviewer's credentials and certification status
- Supervisor review and approval

### 8.3 Coder Qualification Standards

**Continuing Education Requirements:**
- Annual training on updates to ICD-10-CM, HCC models, and compliance expectations
- Specific training on MEAT documentation standards
- Training on CMS-HCC model changes and transitions
- Risk adjustment coding-specific continuing education

**Quality Assurance Standards:**
- Routine audits of coder work (10-20% sample)
- Feedback and retraining when errors identified
- Documentation of quality metrics
- Removal from assignments if error rate exceeds thresholds

**Recent Coder Concerns (OIG Focus):**
- Verification that coders understand boundaries between legitimate capture and aggressive coding
- Confirmation that coders are not under incentive structures that reward unsupported coding
- Verification that coders receive proper clinical context and documentation before assigning codes

## 9. ENCOUNTER DATA COMPLETENESS REQUIREMENTS

### 9.1 Regulatory Framework

**Statutory Requirement:** 42 CFR § 422.310(b)

**Submission Requirements:**
MA organizations must submit to CMS (in accordance with CMS instructions) the data necessary to characterize the context and purposes of each item and service provided to a Medicare enrollee.

**Data Submission Standards:**
- Must conform to CMS requirements for data equivalent to Medicare FFS data
- Must follow all relevant national standards (ANSI 837 5010 format)
- Must be submitted electronically to appropriate CMS contractor
- Must meet CMS' data quality specifications

### 9.2 Encounter Data Submission Process

**Data Submission Methods:**
- Risk Adjustment Processing System (RAPS): Standardized format
- Encounter Data System (EDS): Claims-based format from medical encounters
- ANSI 837 5010 Health Care Claim format

**Submission Deadlines:**
- First Friday in September: Services from 12-month period ending June 30
- First Friday in March: Services from 12-month period ending December 31
- All data must be submitted by 8:00 PM ET on deadline date
- Data submitted after deadline is not used in payment calculations

### 9.3 Data Completeness Standards

**High Completeness Benchmark:**
Research defines high data completeness as:
- Less than 10% missing hospital stays
- Less than ±10% difference in ambulatory and ED visits between encounter data and HEDIS data
- Complete documentation for all submitted diagnosis codes

**Industry Data Quality:**
- Only 83 of 380 MA contracts (21.8%) with 2,500+ enrollees met high completeness standards as of recent assessments
- Significant plan-level variation in completeness
- Home health and nursing home encounter files identified as particularly inaccurate

### 9.4 Medical Record Data Collection Requirements

**Data Collection Process:**
- MA organizations must obtain risk adjustment data from providers, suppliers, physicians, and practitioners
- Data must be collected from actual medical encounters/services
- Data must characterize context and purpose of items and services provided

**Contractual Provisions:**
- MA organizations may include contract provisions requiring providers to submit complete and accurate risk adjustment data
- Financial penalties for incomplete data submission may be included
- Obligation to report encountered data back to providers for validation

### 9.5 Data Validation and Penalties

**Validation Requirements:**
- MA organizations required to submit sample of medical records for CMS validation
- CMS conducts verification sampling
- MAOs required to correct identified deficiencies

**Penalties for False Data:**
- Civil monetary penalties up to $25,000 per affected enrollee
- Assessments for continued deficiencies
- Potential exclusion for willful submission of false data
- OIG may require remediation and overpayment correction

### 9.6 Encounter Data Quality Issues (Recent Findings)

**Current Concerns:**
- Incomplete capture of all patient encounters
- Inaccurate diagnosis coding in submitted encounter data
- Missing clinical context limiting audit validation
- Delays in data submission affecting CMS processing
- Inconsistencies between internal records and submitted encounter data

**OIG Work Plan Items:**
- Ongoing audit of MA encounter data accuracy
- Examination of diagnosis code patterns
- Validation of HEDIS data consistency with submitted encounters

## 10. RECENT DOJ SETTLEMENTS RELATED TO RISK ADJUSTMENT FRAUD

### 10.1 Kaiser Permanente - $556 Million Settlement (January 2026)

**Parties:** Kaiser Permanente affiliates and DOJ/OIG

**Settlement Amount:** $556 million (largest MA fraud settlement ever)

**Allegations:**
- Altered patients' medical records by adding approximately 500,000 diagnoses
- Diagnoses either did not exist or were unrelated to the visit they were connected to
- Additions made months or even a year after the visits using addenda
- Generated approximately $1 billion in unsupported payments from CMS

**Practices at Issue:**
- Classic "one-way audit" - aggressively adding codes but never removing invalid ones
- Post-encounter addenda to diagnoses far removed from the original visit
- Lack of MEAT documentation for submitted diagnoses
- Systematic pattern of upcoding

**Whistleblower Component:**
- Qui tam whistleblowers: Ronda Osinek and James M. Taylor, M.D. (former Kaiser employees)
- Whistleblower award: $95 million

**Settlement Terms:**
- No admission of liability by Kaiser
- Resolves FCA allegations regarding diagnosis coding practices
- Demonstrates DOJ's serious enforcement posture on MA risk adjustment fraud

### 10.2 Cigna - $172 Million Settlement (2023)

**Parties:** Cigna and DOJ/OIG

**Settlement Amount:** $172 million

**Allegations:**
- Submitted false and inaccurate Medicare Advantage diagnostic codes
- Aim: Boost reimbursement rates
- Reported diagnoses based solely on cursory in-home assessments
- Providers did not perform necessary diagnostic testing and imaging
- Cigna knew diagnoses would increase MA payments by making members appear sicker
- Alleged conduct dated back more than a decade

**Corporate Integrity Agreement (CIA):**
- Cigna entered into 5-year CIA with OIG
- Requires implementation of numerous accountability and auditing provisions
- Enhanced reporting and monitoring obligations

**Settlement Framework:**
- Second-largest MA risk adjustment settlement (after Kaiser)
- Clear precedent for DOJ action on diagnosis coding issues

### 10.3 Aetna - $117.7 Million Settlement (March 2026)

**Parties:** Aetna Inc. and DOJ/OIG

**Settlement Amount:** $117.7 million

**Allegations:**
- Violated False Claims Act by submitting/failing to correct inaccurate diagnoses
- Payment Year 2015 chart review program issues
- Hired coders to review MA enrollees' medical records
- Coders identified additional diagnoses submitted to CMS
- Practice yielded higher risk-adjusted payments
- 2018-2023: Submitted/failed to delete inaccurate diagnoses for morbid obesity
- Submitted obesity diagnoses without BMI documentation to substantiate

**Distinctive Element - CIA Refusal:**
- Although settling FCA allegations, Aetna refused Corporate Integrity Agreement
- OIG reserved right to exclude Aetna under separate authority
- Aetna subject to "heightened scrutiny" for 10 years

**Settlement Structure:**
- Settlement with DOJ and OIG
- Covers multiple payment years
- Demonstrates OIG's continuing focus on diagnosis coding practices

### 10.4 UnitedHealth Investigation (Ongoing)

**Status:** Active DOJ investigation (as of July 2025)

**Nature:**
- Qui tam lawsuit alleging fraudulent use of risk-adjustment data
- Alleged fraud through UnitedHealth's chart review program for MA plans
- DOJ intervened in the case

**Government Position:**
- UnitedHealth cooperating with both criminal and civil DOJ requests
- Likely to be "biggest Medicare Advantage fraud probe yet"
- Investigation ongoing, settlement terms not yet determined

### 10.5 Broader Enforcement Context

**FY 2025 Healthcare Fraud Recovery:**
- Total DOJ FCA recoveries: $6.8 billion (FY 2025)
- Healthcare-related settlements: $5.7 billion
- Significant portion tied to MA risk adjustment theories

**DOJ Priority:**
- MA enforcement designated as top priority in re-launched DOJ-HHS False Claims Act Working Group
- MA risk adjustment identified as first focus area
- Pattern of aggressive prosecution with large settlements
- Clear signal that MA organizations should expect heightened scrutiny

### 10.6 Qui Tam Litigation Patterns

**Whistleblower Trends:**
- Recent cases show whistleblower cooperation critical to settlement amounts
- Insiders (physicians, coders, former employees) providing case information
- Whistleblower percentages typically 15-30% of settlement
- Example: Kaiser $95M award to two whistleblowers from $556M settlement

**Implications:**
- Organizations should assume internal awareness of coding issues creates whistleblower risk
- Failure to correct known issues increases FCA and qui tam exposure
- Prompt self-reporting and correction may reduce penalty severity

## 11. COMPREHENSIVE COMPLIANCE FRAMEWORK AND REGULATORY CITATIONS

### 11.1 Key Regulatory Citations

**Statutory Framework:**
- Social Security Act § 1102 (CMS authority)
- Social Security Act § 1128A(a) - Civil Monetary Penalties authority

## 12. RISK ASSESSMENT FOR MA ORGANIZATIONS

### 12.1 High-Risk Areas

Based on recent enforcement actions and OIG guidance, MA organizations should assess risk in:

1. **Diagnosis Code Capture Programs**
   - Risk: Aggressive post-encounter diagnosis capture
   - Mitigation: Limit capture to diagnoses documented in clinical notes, not assumptions

2. **Chart Review Vendors**
   - Risk: Vendor incentive structures rewarding higher diagnosis capture
   - Mitigation: Audit vendor methodologies, ensure independence

3. **One-Way Audit Practices**
   - Risk: Adding codes but never removing invalid ones
   - Mitigation: Implement systematic removal of invalid codes, not just addition

4. **Unsupported High-Dollar HCCs**
   - Risk: HCCs with high risk adjustment impact but minimal documentation
   - Mitigation: Special scrutiny, benchmark against peers

5. **Post-Encounter Addenda**
   - Risk: Diagnoses added months after encounter
   - Mitigation: Establish time limits (e.g., 30 days post-encounter)

6. **Coder Incentive Structures**
   - Risk: Compensation based on diagnoses captured/coded
   - Mitigation: Tie compensation to accuracy, not volume

### 12.2 Compliance Program Audit Checklist

Essential elements to review:

- [ ] Written risk adjustment policies reviewed and updated within 12 months
- [ ] Compliance officer designated with appropriate reporting authority
- [ ] Risk adjustment training conducted for all relevant staff (annual minimum)
- [ ] Pre-submission data audits performed on risk adjustment data
- [ ] Post-submission data audits performed (sample of submitted vs. approved data)
- [ ] High-risk HCC codes flagged and reviewed
- [ ] Coder credentials verified (CRC, CPC, or equivalent)
- [ ] Quality assurance program with documented coder audits
- [ ] Disciplinary guidelines documented and enforced
- [ ] Third-party oversight (vendors, providers) implemented
- [ ] Monthly LEIE screening conducted
- [ ] Overpayment identification and correction process documented
- [ ] Corrective action tracking system implemented
- [ ] Internal reporting procedures with non-retaliation protection

## 13. RECENT ENFORCEMENT TRENDS (2025-2026)

### 13.1 Quantitative Enforcement Data

**FY 2025 Healthcare Fraud Recovery:** $6.8 billion total
- Healthcare-specific: $5.7 billion
- MA risk adjustment: Significant component (Kaiser $556M, Aetna $117.7M)

**RADV Audit Expansion:**
- Contracts audited: Expanded from 60 to 550+
- Coding reviewers: Expanded from 40 to 2,000+
- Payment years in audit: PY 2018-2024 backlog targeted for completion by early 2026

### 13.2 Qualitative Enforcement Patterns

**Prosecutorial Focus Areas:**
1. One-way audits - most aggressive enforcement
2. Post-encounter addenda - significant penalty multiplier
3. Unsupported high-cost HCCs - primary target
4. Absence of MEAT documentation - invalidates majority of sampled codes

**Settlement Trends:**
- Average settlement: $100M-$556M for large organizations
- Corporate Integrity Agreements standard (except Aetna's unusual refusal)
- Whistleblower awards: 15-30% of settlement amounts
- Criminal liability: Not yet pursued in major cases, but DOJ-HHS working group active

### 13.3 Legal Uncertainty (September 2025 Court Ruling)

**Humana v. Becerra Impact:**
- Extrapolation rule validity in question pending appeal
- CMS appealed November 21, 2025
- Likely resolution 6-12 months out
- Plans must prepare for both scenarios

**Implications:**
- If appeal succeeds: Extrapolation methodology reinstated, prior audits may proceed, exposure increases dramatically
- If appeal fails: CMS must reissue rule with better procedural foundation, uncertainty continues
- Either way: RADV audits continuing; extrapolation methodology may be refined but not eliminated

## CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

### Key Findings

1. **Regulatory Intensity:** CMS is dramatically increasing risk adjustment audit activity (from 60 to 550+ contracts, from 40 to 2,000+ reviewers), signaling this remains a top enforcement priority.

2. **False Claims Act Exposure:** Every unsupported diagnosis code constitutes a false claim under FCA, with treble damages plus $11,000-$15,000 per claim penalties. A $1 billion overpayment could generate $3-9 billion in FCA liability.

3. **Documentation Standards:** MEAT requirements (Monitoring, Evaluation, Assessment, Treatment) are non-negotiable. Historical audits invalidate 20-40% of unsupported codes.

4. **Settlement Precedent:** Major settlements ($556M Kaiser, $172M Cigna, $117.7M Aetna) demonstrate DOJ's willingness to pursue aggressive enforcement with substantial penalties.

5. **Methodological Uncertainty:** The September 2025 Humana v. Becerra decision creates procedural uncertainty about extrapolation, but this appears temporary pending appeal. Organizations should assume extrapolation will ultimately be allowed.

6. **Compliance Program Imperative:** OIG's February 2026 guidance update (first since 1999) sets new compliance expectations, particularly regarding risk adjustment controls, third-party oversight, and proactive correction.

### Critical Action Items

- Implement coder quality assurance program with documented audits
- Establish overpayment identification and correction procedures
- Train all relevant staff on MEAT documentation requirements and FCA liability

**Strategic (180+ days):**
- Implement benchmarking analysis of HCC prevalence against national standards and peers
